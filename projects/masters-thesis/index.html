Master's Thesis (Ongoing)

Spatiotemporal Modeling of Urban Heat Risk with Explainable GeoAI

#Urban Heat Island#Explainable AI#Spatiotemporal Modeling#IPCC Risk Framework#SHAP#Remote Sensing

Overview

Urban heat is usually studied as a physical phenomenon — a temperature difference between a city and its surroundings. That framing is incomplete. The IPCC risk framework defines risk as the product of three components: hazard (the physical heat signal), exposure (who and what is in its path), and vulnerability (how sensitive that population and infrastructure is to being harmed). Most remote sensing studies model the physical hazard, and do not take the exposed and vulnerable population in consideration.

This thesis proposes a framework that treats risk as the IPCC defines it. The central claim is that urban heat risk cannot be captured by surface temperature maps alone — it requires the joint modeling of physical hazard, socio-demographic exposure, and social vulnerability, all resolved in space and time.

Status: Proposal defense passed (20/20, Sep 2025). Published papers and defense on the way. The page below reflects the proposal as approved, not preliminary results. Method outputs, calibration findings, and final maps will be added as each phase completes.

Supervisor: Dr. Parastoo Pilehforooshha Department: GIS, K. N. Toosi University of Technology

Why This Problem, and Why Now

Three specific gaps motivate the work.

Gap 1 — Hazard is not risk

Studies of the surface urban heat island (SUHI) overwhelmingly measure the physical temperature and stop there. But high surface temperature over a commercial district with nobody living in it is a very different problem from moderate temperature over an elderly population with poor healthcare access. Conflating the two misdirects policy — cooling a surface doesn’t reduce risk if nobody’s exposed, and the people most at risk are often in areas that don’t show up as the hottest.

The IPCC framework already provides a rigorous alternative: risk emerges from the interaction of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. Applying it to urban heat is not novel in the abstract, but applying it with spatially and temporally resolved data at sub-kilometer scales is. This is the gap this thesis addresses.

Gap 2 — The resolution tradeoff

There is a persistent trade-off in thermal remote sensing between spatial and temporal resolution. MODIS provides LST at 1 km, daily — too coarse to see the street-level variation that determines who is exposed. Landsat 8/9 provides LST at 30 m but only every 8–16 days, too sparse to capture the seasonal dynamics of heat risk. Neither sensor alone can produce the product that risk assessment actually needs: sub-kilometer temperature, resolved across seasons.

The resolution paradox has a solution — machine learning downscaling — but it has not been applied in combination with the full IPCC risk framework. This thesis couples the two.

Gap 3 — The black-box problem

XGBoost and similar models are accurate but opaque. They tell you that NDVI matters for SUHI. They do not tell you how much, whether that relationship is stable across seasons, or whether it varies between two cities with different climates. Policy-makers cannot act on a model whose internal logic is hidden.

Explainable AI — specifically SHAP — turns those models from black boxes into sources of quantitative, per-pixel, per-feature attribution. This is the last piece. It is what converts a prediction into something a city planner can actually use.

Study Areas — Tehran and Singapore

Two climatic and morphological archetypes, deliberately chosen for maximum contrast.

Tehran — arid, mid-latitude, high elevation (~1,200 m), with a dense urban core that experiences intense summer SUHI and severe winter inversions. Aging population in the central districts, uneven access to green space, heterogeneous building stock.

Singapore — tropical, low elevation, high humidity, weak seasonality. High-rise, high-density, with strong urban ventilation corridors and equally strong localized heating around industrial and port areas.

The point of running the same framework on both is not to compare the cities directly — the outputs will be in different units and different magnitudes — but to test whether the framework generalizes. Do the same SHAP drivers emerge? Does the same vulnerability structure hold? If the framework only works on one climate type, it isn’t a framework; it’s a case study. The two-city design is the falsification test.

Data

All raster processing is done in Google Earth Engine. Vector integration and final cartography use ArcGIS Pro.

Hazard layer:

  • Sentinel-2 (spectral indices, land cover)
  • Landsat 8/9 (high-resolution LST for validation, calibration targets)
  • MODIS (daily LST at 1 km — the downscaling source)
  • ERA5-Land (meteorological covariates — air temperature, humidity, wind)
  • SRTM DEM / ALOS AW3D30 (elevation, slope, aspect)
  • Google Earth Engine (compute platform)

Exposure layer:

  • WorldPop (age-stratified, sex-stratified population grids)
  • OSM (buildings, road network)

Vulnerability layer:

  • WorldPop (age structure — children and elderly)
  • OSM (healthcare infrastructure, green/blue space access)
  • Socioeconomic indicators (economic deprivation proxies)

Every dataset is globally available, which is intentional. A framework that depends on non-public data cannot be replicated elsewhere. The outputs may be Tehran- and Singapore-specific, but the pipeline is transferable.

Methodology — Five Phases

Phase 1 — LST Downscaling

A Random Forest model in GEE learns the relationship between coarse MODIS LST (1 km, daily) and high-resolution predictors: Sentinel-2 spectral indices, DEM-derived topographic variables, and ERA5-Land meteorology. The trained model is then applied to the high-resolution predictors to generate a sub-kilometer, seasonally resolved LST product.

Validation is against Landsat 8/9 thermal bands — held out entirely from training — with RMSE and bias reported per season and per land cover type.

In parallel, Local Climate Zone (LCZ) classification is performed using the standard Stewart–Oke scheme (LCZ 1–10 and LCZ A–G). LCZ provides a physically meaningful stratification of the urban surface that later phases use for interpretation.

Phase 2 — Spatiotemporal Data Cube

All inputs — downscaled SUHI, atmospheric variables, Sentinel-2 indices, three-dimensional morphology (building height, sky view factor, frontal area index) — are assembled into a unified four-dimensional structure: (X, Y, T, Features). Normalization is applied feature-wise to keep the cube consistent across seasons and sites.

This is not a data storage decision. It’s a modeling decision. The cube makes it possible to ask questions that are awkward to ask otherwise — how does the ranking of heat drivers change between summer and winter? — without re-running an entire pipeline per season.

Phase 3 — Hazard Modeling and Explainability

XGBoost is trained on the data cube to model the nonlinear relationship between all predictors and the SUHI hazard. Model performance is assessed with spatial cross-validation, not random splits, to avoid optimistic bias.

The fitted model is then interpreted with SHAP. Two products come out of this phase:

  • Per-pixel SHAP values — the contribution of each feature to the hazard prediction at that location
  • Global feature importance ranked by season — how much albedo, NDVI, elevation, built height, and the rest actually contribute, and whether that ranking is stable across the year

The temporal stability question matters. If elevation is the dominant driver in winter but vegetation is dominant in summer, the policy prescription is completely different for the two seasons. That distinction is invisible in a static importance plot.

Phase 4 — IPCC Risk Integration

The hazard layer (Phase 1) combines with exposure and vulnerability under the IPCC risk formulation:

  • Hazard (H) — downscaled SUHI, per season
  • Exposure (E) — population density and asset density, derived from WorldPop and OSM
  • Vulnerability (V) — the sum of Sensitivity (S) and Adaptive Capacity (AC):
    • Sensitivity — age structure, population density, economic deprivation
    • Adaptive Capacity — healthcare access, green and blue space access, socioeconomic status

The functional form of the risk combination — product, sum, weighted aggregation — is a substantive modeling choice and is treated as one. Sensitivity analysis on the weighting scheme will be part of the final results.

Phase 5 — Comparative Spatiotemporal Analysis

Final risk maps are analyzed for both cities across the seasonal cycle. Two questions drive this phase:

  1. Do the same drivers of hazard dominate in Tehran and Singapore, or does climate regime fundamentally change what drives the SUHI signal?
  2. Does the risk structure — the relationship between hazard, exposure, and vulnerability — hold across the two cities, or does it collapse under climatic contrast?

The comparative framing is what makes the framework falsifiable. If Tehran and Singapore produce structurally similar risk patterns, the framework is validated as transferable. If they don’t, the reasons why are themselves informative.

What Makes This Different

Four claims of novelty, each addressing one of the gaps above:

  1. Sub-kilometer, seasonally resolved SUHI produced by ML-based downscaling coupled with multi-source remote sensing — the resolution combination that neither MODIS nor Landsat provides alone.
  2. Explainable GeoAI applied to hazard attribution — SHAP is used not as decoration but as the mechanism for quantifying feature contribution, season by season.
  3. An integrated spatiotemporal risk model built entirely on globally available data, so the pipeline can be re-run elsewhere without data-access barriers.
  4. A comparative framework across two contrasting climates — Tehran and Singapore — designed to test generalizability rather than demonstrate one city’s specifics.

Expected Outputs

  • Spatiotemporal SUHI hazard maps for Tehran and Singapore, sub-kilometer, seasonal
  • Spatiotemporal urban heat risk (UHR) maps at the same resolution
  • SHAP attribution plots and per-season feature importance rankings
  • A reproducible pipeline for applying the framework to other cities

Tools and Environment

  • Google Earth Engine — raster processing and ML downscaling at scale
  • Python — XGBoost, SHAP, data cube construction, statistical analysis
  • ArcGIS Pro — vector integration and cartography

Timeline

The five phases span approximately twelve months of work. Phase 1 is complete and Phase 2 is in progress. Phases 3 through 5 are scheduled sequentially, with the final defense targeted for the end of the 2025–2026 academic year.

Results — downscaled LST validation metrics, SHAP attribution maps, and final risk products — will be published here as each phase completes.
