Multi-Criteria Waste Disposal Site Selection in Minoodasht, Golestan

A weighted overlay framework integrating environmental, economic, and social criteria in ArcGIS Pro.

#ArcGIS Pro#Multi-Criteria Analysis#Waste Management#Spatial Planning#Euclidean Distance

Overview

Waste disposal siting is a multi-criteria spatial decision problem. A site must satisfy environmental regulations, minimize economic cost, and remain socially acceptable — three objectives that frequently conflict. Meeting one at the expense of another typically means the candidate fails regulatory review or public consultation. The work below is a full multi-criteria evaluation (MCE) workflow for locating waste disposal sites in Minoodasht, a district in Golestan Province, northeastern Iran.

The project was carried out under the supervision of Dr. Pilehforooshha as part of the Urban Planning course at K.N. Toosi University of Technology. The scope is deliberately narrow, but the methodology is the same one used in operational site selection studies: build a criteria set, standardize every layer to a common scale, apply weights, and mask out areas that are legally or physically off-limits.

Criteria Framework

The site-selection criteria are organized into three categories. Each carries equal analytical weight in structure — the weight applied to individual criteria varies, but every category is represented.

Environmental

  • Distance from rivers and surface water bodies
  • Annual precipitation
  • Proximity to protected ecological areas (treated as a constraint, not a weighted criterion)

Economic

  • Elevation
  • Slope
  • Distance from the road network

Social

  • Distance from urban centers
  • Distance from rural settlements

Constraints vs. Criteria

Two distinct mechanisms are at play. Criteria are continuous variables that push a candidate site up or down in the final suitability score — a site is never fully disqualified by a criterion, only penalized. Constraints are binary. If a pixel falls inside a constraint, its final score is forced to zero regardless of how well it scores on every other dimension.

In this project, protected ecological areas are the only constraint. Every other layer contributes to the score. The constraint is enforced multiplicatively at the end of the pipeline, which guarantees that no weighting scheme can override it.

Data Preparation

All inputs arrive in WGS84 geographic coordinates. Before any analysis, two standardization steps are applied.

Projection to UTM Zone 40N. Euclidean distance operations only produce accurate results in a projected coordinate system where one map unit equals one meter. Geographic coordinates distort distance, especially at the study area’s latitude, so every layer is reprojected to UTM Zone 40N before any distance operation is run.

Resampling to a 50 m grid. The native DEM has a much finer resolution than the rest of the dataset. To prevent millions of near-zero-value pixels from dominating the overlay, every raster is resampled to a uniform 50 m cell size. This is a deliberate trade-off: at 10 m the final raster would carry far more detail than the input criteria can justify, and processing time grows quadratically without adding analytical value.

All rasters are subsequently clipped to the Minoodasht study area boundary using Extract by Mask so that every layer shares the same extent.

Processing Pipeline

Slope is derived from the resampled DEM using the Spatial Analyst Slope tool. Slope enters the suitability model because flat sites reduce construction cost and lower the risk of leachate runoff.

Euclidean Distance is applied to five vector layers — rivers, roads, fault lines, urban centers, and rural settlements — producing five continuous distance rasters. Each is computed against the same 50 m grid so they stack cleanly in the final overlay.

Precipitation is interpolated from meteorological stations using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW). IDW was chosen over Kriging because the station network is too sparse to support a reliable variogram fit. The input stations are reprojected to UTM 40N before interpolation, and the operation is run over the entire study area without a mask so that edge effects at the boundary are minimized.

Reclassification

Every continuous raster carries different units — meters, degrees, millimeters per year. To combine them, all eight criteria are reclassified onto a common 1–10 integer scale using Reclassify. The direction of the scoring is not uniform:

Criterion Scoring direction Rationale
Slope Lower slope → higher score Flat terrain reduces construction cost
Elevation Lower elevation → higher score Cheaper access and infrastructure
Distance from rivers Farther → higher score Prevents contamination of surface water
Distance from roads Farther → higher score Discourages illegal dumping via direct road access
Distance from cities Farther → higher score Public-health buffer
Distance from villages Farther → higher score Public-health buffer
Precipitation Lower → higher score Reduced leachate generation
Distance from faults Farther → higher score Seismic risk mitigation

Protected areas are reclassified through Polygon to Raster into a binary surface — 1 for legally permitted land, 0 for protected areas.

Weighted Overlay

The composite suitability surface is built with Raster Calculator, using a weighted sum of the eight reclassified criteria multiplied by the binary constraint:

((0.10 * ReclassDEM) + (0.10 * ReclassPrecip) + (0.20 * ReclassDistCity) + (0.20 * ReclassDistVillage) + (0.15 * ReclassDistRoads) + (0.15 * ReclassDistRiver) + (0.05 * ReclassDistFault) + (0.05 * ReclassSlope)) * ProtectionMask

The weights reflect the relative importance of each criterion for this specific context. Social acceptance — encoded in the distance-to-settlement criteria — carries 40% of the total weight because waste siting decisions in populated districts are almost always decided on social grounds. Environmental criteria account for 40% split across rivers, precipitation, and the protected-area constraint. Economic criteria take the remaining 20%.

The final multiplication by the protection mask is what enforces the constraint. Any pixel inside a protected area scores zero regardless of its weighted sum.

Results

The weighted overlay produces a continuous suitability surface in the range [0, 1]. This is reclassified into three interpretable tiers:

  • Suitable — composite score in the upper band; site satisfies all criteria with margin
  • Semi-suitable — mid-range score; usable with mitigation measures
  • Unsuitable — low score or falls inside a protected area

The final map was exported as a layout with a legend, scale bar, north arrow, and coordinate system annotation so that it can be read by non-specialist stakeholders without additional explanation.

Notes

The pipeline runs on a single district, but nothing in it is scale-specific. The same eight criteria, the same reclassification ranges, and the same weighting scheme can be lifted to any similar study area by swapping the input layers. The two steps that matter most for reproducibility are the coordinate-system standardization and the resampling — skip either, and the composite surface silently loses accuracy in ways that are hard to detect after the fact.
