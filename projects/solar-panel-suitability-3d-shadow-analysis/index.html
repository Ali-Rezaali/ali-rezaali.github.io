Solar Panel Suitability — 3D Shadow Analysis and Fuzzy Multi-Criteria Evaluation

Ranking 15 candidate sites for photovoltaic installation by combining cumulative shadow volume, daily shadow frequency, and terrain suitability in a weighted overlay.

#3D GIS#Suitability Modeling#Fuzzy Logic#Solar Energy#ArcGIS Pro#Shadow Analysis

Overview

Solar panel placement is a problem with two independent constraints that must be satisfied simultaneously. A site needs to receive direct sunlight for as many hours of the day as possible — an orientation and terrain problem. And it needs to be free of shadow cast by nearby obstructions — a three-dimensional geometry problem that requires a full 3D scene to solve correctly.

This project builds both constraints into a single suitability model. Fifteen candidate sites were simulated within the study area, the full 3D scene (buildings, trees, infrastructure) was reconstructed in ArcGIS Pro, and the shadow behavior of each candidate was evaluated over a twelve-hour daylight window. Terrain orientation and slope were folded in through fuzzy logic, and the three components were combined in a weighted sum.

3D Scene Reconstruction

The entire scene was built from the CAD layer stack used in the accompanying Advanced GIS exercises. This was not a trivial extrusion — different feature classes required different treatment.

Point features (orchards, palms, single trees, telecom towers, urban lamps, monitoring towers, water/gas/oil tanks) were represented with 3D Marker symbols at realistic scale. Representing a telecom tower as a 2D point with an extrusion would have been visually unconvincing and would have produced incorrect shadow footprints.

Linear features (fences, walls, barbed wire) could not be extruded meaningfully from their native geometry, so they were converted to sample lines and rendered at a fixed height of 5 m. This is a simplification, but for shadow-casting purposes a 5 m linear barrier is a reasonable representation of a 2–3 m wall plus the shadow it casts at low sun angles.

Polygon features — buildings, urban blocks, service structures (post offices, courts, parking) — were extruded using Shape.Z where available. Where no elevation field was present in the source data, a fixed 10 m extrusion was applied uniformly. This was the only reasonable option given that the CAD file did not carry per-building heights, but it means very tall and very short buildings both appear as 10 m boxes.

Surface features — highways, roads, dirt paths, rivers, canals, drains, seas, lakes, pools, orchards, vineyards, palm groves, forests — were rendered flat on the DSM surface. They were not extruded, because none of them cast meaningful shadows at the scale of interest.

Excluded features — bridges, tunnels, tunnels underpasses, garbage dumps, ruins, non-casting towers, power lines (no shadow at the study scale), rock outcrops, embankments, docking structures, and boundary markers — were dropped from the 3D model entirely. Adding them would have multiplied complexity without improving the shadow prediction.

The final DSM was produced by converting all extruded features to a Multipatch Feature Class, then rasterizing to a Digital Surface Model at 1 m resolution.

Sun Shadow Volume — Cumulative Shadow Analysis

The first shadow metric captures how much shadow accumulates on each pixel over the full analysis window.

Parameter Value
Start 5/25/2024 5:00 AM
End 5/25/2024 5:00 PM
Interval 1 hour
Total iterations 12

Sun Shadow Volume projects the sun’s position at each of the 12 hourly intervals, computes the shadow footprint of every obstruction in the scene, and accumulates the result into a raster where each cell’s value is the number of hours of shadow that cell experiences.

The result is a spatial map of total shadow burden. A pixel at value 0 never fell in shadow; a pixel at value 12 was in shadow for the entire day.

For the suitability model, this is inverted — lower shadow is better — and normalized to a 0–1 scale.

Sun Shadow Frequency — Daily Shadow Frequency

The second metric asks a different question. Instead of “how much shadow accumulated?”, it asks “in how many of the 12 hourly snapshots was this pixel in shadow?”

Sun Shadow Frequency produces a raster where each pixel’s value is a count of shadow events. A cell at value 13 means it was in shadow 13 times across the analysis window — which for a 12-hour window is not possible for a single snapshot, but is possible when the same cell overlaps multiple shadow footprints from different sun angles.

The distinction between Volume and Frequency matters. Volume captures duration-weighted shadow, which is what you want for solar panel feasibility. Frequency captures the number of distinct shadow events, which is more relevant for understanding how many times per day the site’s production would be interrupted.

Both were computed and both were included in the weighted overlay, with different weights. The report’s methodology gives Frequency a higher weight (50%) than Volume (30%), on the reasoning that intermittent shadowing (many short events) is more damaging to panel productivity than sustained shadowing (one long event) — the latter can be partly mitigated by orientation, while the former causes repeated brownouts.

A note on scale: Sun Shadow Volume cannot be run for windows longer than about 12 hours in a single pass without risk of overflow or memory issues at 1 m resolution. For multi-day or seasonal analyses, Sun Shadow Frequency is the appropriate metric, and Volume becomes a visual aid rather than a quantitative input.

Topographic Analysis — Fuzzy Slope and Aspect

The third component of the suitability model is terrain orientation. Two raster inputs:

Slope — flatter is better. Panels on steep slopes require adjustable mounting and are subject to more self-shading. The fuzzy membership function was Gaussian, centered on 0° and decaying with slope.

Aspect — for the northern hemisphere, panels facing south receive maximum annual insolation. In the study area, the optimum orientation was identified as southeast (135°). The fuzzy membership function was also Gaussian, centered on 135° with a moderate spread to allow some tolerance.

Both memberships were combined with a Fuzzy Overlay (AND) operation so that a pixel must satisfy both conditions to score highly. A flat north-facing slope and a south-facing cliff would both score low; only flat southeast-facing terrain scores high.

Final Weighted Sum

The three processed layers were combined in Weighted Sum with the following weights:

Component Weight
Sun Shadow Volume (inverted) 30%
Sun Shadow Frequency (inverted) 50%
Terrain (slope + aspect fuzzy overlay) 20%

The result is a continuous suitability raster. Higher values indicate sites that receive more sunlight, experience fewer shadow events, and are oriented favorably for panel installation.

Ranking the Candidate Sites

The 15 pre-placed candidate points were scored by extracting the suitability raster value at each point with Extract Multi Values to Points. The points were then sorted in descending order of suitability, and the top three were identified:

Rank Object ID X Y Suitability
1 13 731,728 4,165,611 0.800
2 1 732,439 4,165,265 0.800
3 3 733,297 4,165,146 0.800

The three-way tie at 0.800 is worth noting. The candidate points were simulated rather than surveyed, so they do not systematically cover the study area. Many unoccupied sites in the raster have suitability values equal to or higher than these three — the top-ranked points simply happen to be the ones that were included in the simulation. The ranking is meaningful as a demonstration of the pipeline, not as a definitive site recommendation.

A proper site selection study would generate candidate locations from the suitability raster itself — taking the top N local maxima, or sampling randomly across high-suitability regions — rather than starting from a fixed set of points.

Deliverables

Four output rasters, each capturing a different aspect of the analysis:

  • Sun Shadow Volume Footprints (2D) — cumulative shadow accumulation over the 12-hour window
  • Sun Shadow Frequency (2D) — count of shadow events per pixel
  • Suitability Map (2D) — final weighted overlay, continuous values
  • Sun Shadow Volume (3D) — cumulative shadow projected onto the 3D scene for visual inspection

Takeaways

Shadow modeling is genuinely three-dimensional. Attempting this analysis in 2D — with footprint polygons and manual sun angle calculation — would have produced significantly different rankings, particularly for sites adjacent to tall buildings where shadow behavior changes minute-by-minute as the sun moves.

Cumulative metrics and frequency metrics measure different things. Volume tells you how much shadow a site accumulates. Frequency tells you how many times per day its production is interrupted. For a solar panel feasibility study, Frequency deserves the higher weight because intermittent shadow is more disruptive than sustained shadow.

The suitability map is the deliverable, not the point ranking. The three ranked sites are one possible answer; the raster is the complete answer. Any downstream user of this analysis should regenerate their candidate points from the raster rather than consuming the pre-ranked list.
