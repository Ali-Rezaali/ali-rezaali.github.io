Analytical Photogrammetry — Polynomial Transforms, DLT, and Pointwise Methods

Three exercises on the mathematical core of photogrammetry: global polynomial transforms and their accuracy ceiling, direct linear transformation with a full 11-coefficient camera model, and pointwise local correction using moving average and weighted distance averaging.

#Photogrammetry#DLT#Polynomial Transformation#Camera Calibration#GeoReferencing#Pointwise Methods

Overview

Three reports from the Analytical Photogrammetry course at K. N. Toosi University of Technology, all built around a single question: how do you establish the mathematical relationship between a 2D image plane and a 3D ground coordinate system.

Each report takes a different approach to the same problem. The first uses global polynomial transforms, easy to implement, easy to interpret, but fundamentally limited by their global nature. The second uses Direct Linear Transformation, which folds the entire projective geometry of the camera into a single set of linear equations. The third layers local corrections on top of the global polynomial estimate using weighted residuals from nearby control points.

The progression is deliberate. Global methods are simple but imprecise. DLT is more rigorous but sensitive to control point distribution. Pointwise methods trade theoretical elegance for local accuracy. Knowing which to reach for, and when, is the point of all three.

Each report is available as a Colab notebook with the full implementation.

Part 1 — Global Polynomial Transform

The dataset is 13 image points with digitizer coordinates (x, y) and corresponding ground projection coordinates (E, N) in UTM. The goal is to fit a polynomial that maps every image pixel to its ground location.

Control point selection

Before fitting anything, the spatial distribution of the 13 points has to be examined. An unevenly distributed control network biases the fit toward wherever the points are clustered.

Image coordinates (left) and ground coordinates (right) for the 13 reference points.

Points 2, 7, and 10 sit at three of the corners of the point cloud and give the best spatial spread. They are pulled out as check points (independent control points, ICPs), leaving 10 points for the actual fit. The three check points never participate in estimating the polynomial. Their only job is to expose how well the model generalizes to locations it has not seen.

The three polynomial models

Two equations are fitted simultaneously, one for each ground coordinate:

E = a₀ + a₁x + a₂y + a₃xy + a₄x² + a₅y² + a₆x²y + a₇xy² + a₈x³ + a₉y³
N = b₀ + b₁x + b₂y + b₃xy + b₄x² + b₅y² + b₆x²y + b₇xy² + b₈x³ + b₉y³

The three cases evaluated:

Model Terms Coefficients per axis
Affine constant + linear 3
Quadratic affine + squared + cross 6
Cubic quadratic + cubed + x²y + xy² 10

Each model is fitted with least squares on the 10 control points, then evaluated on the three withheld check points.

Results

The RMSE on the check points:

Model RMSE
Affine 17.04
Quadratic 22.91
Cubic 81.01

The affine transform wins decisively, and the accuracy gets worse as more terms are added. This is the counterintuitive result that makes the exercise worth doing.

Why higher-order terms hurt

Adding polynomial terms increases the model’s flexibility, but flexibility is not the same as accuracy. With only 10 control points, every additional term is another parameter that has to be pinned down by fewer observations per parameter. The 10-term cubic model has exactly as many unknowns as it has control points, which means it can interpolate perfectly through every control point while simultaneously distorting the space between them.

At the check points, that distortion becomes visible. The model is fitting noise.

This is the same phenomenon as overfitting in machine learning, and it has a geometric interpretation: the polynomial surface becomes “warped” to accommodate each control point individually, and the space between points becomes a polynomial extrapolation that has no data to constrain it.

Warp behavior for affine, quadratic, and cubic transforms. The higher-order terms introduce distortion that the data cannot constrain.

The other issue is conditioning. Polynomial bases of degree 3 and higher produce design matrices with highly correlated columns (the terms x² and x³ are nearly collinear for closely spaced x values). This leads to an ill-conditioned normal matrix and numerical instability in the least-squares solution. Even when the model fits the training data well, the coefficient values themselves can be unreliable.

The takeaway

For small control networks with modest geometric distortion, the affine transform is the right answer. Its lower parameter count means it captures the dominant systematic error between the two coordinate systems (translation, rotation, scale, and shear) without inventing structure that isn’t there.

Part 2 — Direct Linear Transformation

DLT takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of approximating the mapping with polynomial terms, it recovers the projective geometry of the camera directly.

The setup: 42 reference points, each with pixel coordinates (r, c), ground coordinates (X, Y, Z), and known camera properties. The pixel coordinates are first converted into photogrammetric image coordinates using the camera calibration parameters:

[x]   [ ds    0   -ds·c_p ] [c]
[y] = [ 0   -ds    ds·r_p ] [r]
[-f]   [ 0     0      -f  ] [1]

where ds is the pixel size in millimeters, (c_p, r_p) is the principal point, and f is the focal length.

The DLT equations

The mapping from ground to image is captured by 11 coefficients, L₁ through L₁₁:

x = (L₁X + L₂Y + L₃Z + L₄) / (L₉X + L₁₀Y + L₁₁Z + 1)
y = (L₅X + L₆Y + L₇Z + L₈) / (L₉X + L₁₀Y + L₁₁Z + 1)

Eleven unknowns, and each reference point contributes two equations. Six well-distributed points would be technically enough to solve the system, but in practice you want many more to stabilize the estimate against measurement noise.

Control point selection

The 42 points were inspected for distribution. A good control point has to satisfy several conditions:

  • Sharp and unambiguously identifiable in the image
  • Not on a flat surface with no distinctive features
  • Positioned toward the corners of the frame, where lens distortion is easiest to model
  • Distributed evenly across the image footprint

The 15 points selected as control (spread across all four quadrants of the image plus the center) were removed from the point set. The remaining 27 points became the independent check points.

Control points marked on the aerial frame. The distribution deliberately covers corners and mid-frame in all four quadrants.

What DLT captures

Unlike the polynomial approach, DLT is a physical model. The 11 coefficients encode:

  • Camera position and orientation (the extrinsic parameters)
  • Focal length and pixel scale (the intrinsic parameters)
  • The full projective geometry of the imaging system

This means DLT automatically handles perspective distortion and image tilt, both of which the affine transform cannot model. For a full 3D reconstruction from a single image plus ground control, DLT is the standard tool.

The tradeoff is sensitivity. DLT assumes a perfect pinhole camera with no lens distortion. Any radial or tangential distortion in the actual lens leaks into the residuals and can be absorbed by the coefficients in ways that produce good fits on the control points but poor extrapolation elsewhere.

Part 3 — Pointwise Polynomial Correction

The third report attacks the same 13-point dataset from Part 1 but takes a different strategy. Instead of trying to find one global polynomial that fits everything, it fits a global polynomial (affine) and then applies local corrections on top of it, where the corrections are computed from the residuals of nearby control points.

The idea

Global polynomial transforms leave systematic residual patterns. In areas where the control network is sparse, the polynomial estimate drifts. Pointwise correction exploits the fact that this drift is locally smooth. If you know how the polynomial is wrong at each control point, you can interpolate that error to nearby unknown points and subtract it out.

Two interpolation methods are compared.

Method 1 — Moving Average

For each unknown point, fit a local affine model to the residuals (dX, dY) at the surrounding control points:

dXᵢ = A₀ + A₁Xᵢ + A₂Yᵢ
dYᵢ = B₀ + B₁Xᵢ + B₂Yᵢ

The coefficients are estimated from all 10 control points, then the corrections are evaluated at the unknown point’s location. This is essentially a low-order polynomial fit to the residual field.

Method 2 — Weighted Distance Average

A simpler approach that uses inverse distance weighting:

dXᵤ = Σ (wᵢ · dXᵢ) / Σ wᵢ
dYᵤ = Σ (wᵢ · dYᵢ) / Σ wᵢ
wᵢ = 1 / dᵢᵏ

where dᵢ is the distance from the unknown point to control point i and k is a tuning exponent (typically 2). Points close to the unknown location get high weight; distant points fade out.

Applying the corrections

The final coordinates combine the global estimate with the local correction:

X_U_final = X_U_global_polynomial + dX_U
Y_U_final = Y_U_global_polynomial + dY_U

The result should be closer to the true ground position than the global estimate alone, especially near the control network where the correction field is well constrained.

Tradeoffs

The moving average method produces smooth corrections but can overshoot if the residuals have high-frequency structure that the local affine cannot capture. The weighted distance average is more direct but inherits whatever noise is in the residuals of the individual control points, weighted by distance.

Both methods work best when the control network is dense and evenly distributed. When the nearest control point is far from the unknown location, neither method has enough information to correct the estimate, and the correction approaches zero regardless of which formula you use.

Comparison and Conclusion

Method Fit quality on check points Cost
Global Polynomial (affine) Good when control network is small One matrix inversion
Global Polynomial (higher order) Worse than affine, prone to overfitting Same cost, more parameters
DLT Best for full projective geometry Requires 3D ground control
Pointwise correction Improves GP estimate locally Two-pass computation

The unifying lesson across all three reports: model complexity should match the information content of the data. A 10-term polynomial fitted to 10 control points has no room for error checking, and the resulting coefficients are numerically unstable. An 11-coefficient DLT model is appropriate when the physics of the camera system demand it, but not otherwise. A pointwise correction is only as good as the density of the surrounding control network.

Choosing the right method is a matter of understanding which distortions actually dominate in a given application. Lens distortion and image tilt push you toward DLT. Sparse control networks and low-order systematic error push you toward affine with pointwise correction. Overcomplicated models always look good on paper and behave badly in the field.
