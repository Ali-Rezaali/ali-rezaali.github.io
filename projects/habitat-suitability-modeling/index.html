Fuzzy Habitat Suitability Modeling for Mountain Lions in California

A Mamdani Fuzzy Inference System built on GIS and remote sensing to map Puma concolor habitat across California — and to test whether the state's protected areas actually cover the terrain this apex predator needs.

#Fuzzy Logic#GIS#Remote Sensing#Habitat Suitability#Puma concolor#Conservation

Overview

Mountain lions (Puma concolor) are apex predators whose survival depends on large, connected territories. In California, urbanization, road networks, and agricultural expansion have fragmented those territories into isolated patches. Understanding which patches still work — and which are on the edge of working — is a prerequisite for any serious conservation planning.

This project builds a habitat suitability model for California using a Mamdani Fuzzy Inference System. Eight environmental variables feed the system, each characterized by linguistic membership functions rather than hard thresholds. The output is a suitability raster scored from 0 to 100, which I then cross-referenced against the state’s protected areas to ask a specific question: how much of what California protects is actually suitable for the species it’s nominally protecting?

Headline finding: 46.34% of the land inside California’s protected areas is unsuitable for mountain lion habitat. Only 24.25% is genuinely suitable. The mismatch is large enough to matter.

Why Fuzzy Logic Instead of a Hard Threshold

The problem with conventional habitat suitability models is that they draw binary lines where ecology doesn’t. A slope of 20° and a slope of 21° are treated as different categories if the threshold sits at 20.5. A slope of 19° and a slope of 20° are treated as identical. Real habitat doesn’t work that way — suitability degrades gradually across each variable, not in steps.

Fuzzy Inference Systems handle this naturally. Each input variable is described by overlapping membership functions — low, medium, high — where a single pixel can simultaneously be “70% medium slope” and “30% high slope”. Rules combine these memberships across variables, and defuzzification reduces the aggregated fuzzy output to a single crisp score.

Mamdani FIS is the specific variant used here. It’s the most interpretable of the fuzzy system families: every rule is a human-readable statement of the form “if slope is high AND elevation is high AND distance to roads is far, then suitability is high”. You can read the rule base and see exactly what the model believes. That transparency matters for conservation work, where recommendations have to be defensible to land managers and policy analysts who aren’t modelers.

Study Area

California spans roughly 32.5°N to 42°N latitude and 114.1°W to 124.4°W longitude — an area of about 424,000 km² covering coastlines, deserts, the Central Valley, and the Sierra Nevada. The state’s California Department of Fish and Wildlife divides it into seven administrative regions (Northern, North Central, Bay Delta, Central, South Coast, Inland Deserts, and the marine region), which provided the boundary reference for this study.

The landscape diversity is what makes California a useful test case: coastal chaparral, inland conifer forests, Mojave Desert, and the agricultural Central Valley all appear within the same jurisdictional boundary. A suitability model that works here has demonstrated it can handle environmental heterogeneity.

Data

All layers are publicly available. That was a deliberate constraint — habitat models that depend on licensed data can’t be audited or reproduced by the people who need them.

Prey data. 40,830 occurrence records across the 11 known prey species of Puma concolor, aggregated from GBIF. Species include mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus), Columbian black-tailed deer, elk, bighorn sheep, beaver, gray fox, raccoon, brush rabbit, black-tailed jackrabbit, coyote, and wild turkey. The dominant prey signal is mule deer — they carry the highest observation density and appear to drive cougar distribution in most of the state.

Verification data. 7,137 Puma concolor occurrence records from GBIF, used as an independent check that the suitability map reflects areas where the species is actually documented.

Topography. SRTM digital elevation model at 30 m resolution. Three derived layers: elevation (m), slope (degrees), and aspect (compass bearing, resampled to four cardinal categories for the FIS).

Land cover. NLCD 2021 California subset at 30 m. The 16 land cover classes were assigned weighted suitability scores based on how well each provides cover and hunting grounds (see table below).

Proximity layers. Euclidean distance rasters from roads, water bodies, prey locations, and protected area boundaries. Computed statewide, then sampled to the study extent.

The Fuzzy Inference System

Each input variable is described by a set of membership functions. Elevation and slope use Gaussian plus trapezoidal shapes — Gaussian for the intermediate categories where gradual degradation is expected, trapezoidal for the extremes where anything above a certain threshold is equally good.

Elevation: Low (Gaussian centered at 0 m, σ = 500), Medium (Gaussian centered at 1500 m, σ = 400), High Gaussian (centered at 3000 m, σ = 700), High Flat (trapezoidal plateau above 3000 m).

Slope: Low (Gaussian centered at 0°, σ = 5°), Medium (Gaussian centered at 20°, σ = 5°), High Gaussian (centered at 35°, σ = 5°), High Flat (trapezoidal plateau above 35°).

Aspect: South (trapezoidal over ~135°–225°), intermediate transitional band, North (tight trapezoidal around 0°–22.5°).

Distances to roads, prey, water, protected areas: Close (Gaussian centered at 0 m, σ = 300 m), Medium (Gaussian centered at 2500 m, σ = 700 m), Far (Gaussian centered at 5000 m, σ = 500 m), Far Flat (trapezoidal plateau beyond 5000 m).

Suitability (output): Low (triangular 0–50), Medium (triangular 25–75), High (triangular 50–100).

Land cover is treated as a per-pixel weight, not a fuzzy input. Each NLCD class maps to a fixed score from 0 to 1:

This is the piece of the model that carries the most expert judgment. Forest cover scores highest because it provides both stalking cover and prey habitat. Shrub and scrub scores moderately — usable but not ideal. All developed classes score near zero. The scoring is subjective, and I’ll return to that in the limitations.

Rule Generation

The rule base was constructed programmatically. With eight input variables and up to four linguistic categories each, the full combinatorial space is large, so rules were generated from a preference scoring system rather than enumerated by hand.

Each linguistic category is assigned a preference value (High slope → 3, Medium → 2, Low → 1, with inverted scoring for distance to roads where Far is preferred). Those values are summed across all eight variables, and the total determines the rule’s output: Highly Suitable above 20, Suitable above 15, Moderate above 10, Unsuitable at or below 10.

The full rule set is written to a text file and loaded into the FIS at runtime. Doing this by hand would have produced fewer rules but arguably better ones — a domain expert wouldn’t necessarily weight “high elevation” and “close to prey” as equally important. The linear weighting is a simplification that I’ll flag as a real limitation.

Results

The FIS was applied pixel-by-pixel across the California extent at 30 m resolution, producing a continuous suitability raster scored 0 to 100. Three defuzzification methods were tested — Centroid, Bisector of Area, and Mean of Maximum — and the final map uses Mean of Maximum because it handled the trimodal output distributions most cleanly.

The spatial pattern is what you’d hope for: high suitability concentrated in the Sierra Nevada, the northern Coast Ranges, and the Klamath region. Low suitability across the Central Valley, the Mojave, and the densely developed coastal strip between Los Angeles and San Diego. This matches the general distribution of confirmed Puma concolor observations from GBIF, which is a useful sanity check.

The interesting results come from the cross-reference against protected areas.

Accuracy Assessment

Traditional accuracy metrics — RMSE against ground truth, ROC curves, confusion matrices — weren’t available here because there’s no publicly accessible ground-truth suitability dataset. Habitat data in California is either licensed or aggregated at resolutions too coarse to be useful for pixel-level validation.

Rather than skip validation entirely, I used a proxy: protected areas. The logic is that areas formally designated as protected should, on average, be more suitable for wildlife than unprotected land. If the suitability map disagrees with that assumption, one of the two is wrong — either the model is miscalibrated, or the protected area network includes land that isn’t actually serving its conservation purpose.

The answer turned out to be: both, in different places.

Class Overlapping Area Pixel Count
Suitable 24.25% 48,424
Semi-suitable 29.41% 58,725
Unsuitable 46.34% 92,540
Total 100% 199,689

Nearly half of the land inside California’s protected areas scores as unsuitable for mountain lion habitat. This isn’t a modeling artifact — the numbers are consistent with what other researchers have found about protected area networks. Boundaries get drawn around what’s politically feasible and administratively convenient, not around what’s ecologically coherent. Some of the protected land is degraded, some is at elevation or in vegetation types that don’t support the species, and some is too close to urban edges to function as real habitat.

The 29.41% classified as semi-suitable is the interesting middle ground. These areas have partial habitat value — they can serve as movement corridors even if they don’t provide full-time habitat. From a conservation planning perspective, this is where the highest marginal return on investment lives. Restore these corridors and you connect the 24% of fully suitable land into something that actually functions as a metapopulation.

Limitations

Three things deserve honest flagging.

No ground-truth validation. The protected-area overlap assessment is a proxy, not a direct test. It tells you whether the model agrees with an assumption about how protected areas work — not whether the model is right about habitat suitability in a physical sense. A proper validation would require independently derived occurrence data held out from model training, which the GBIF dataset could support but which I did not implement.

Rule base is linear. The rule generation script weights every variable equally in its preference scoring. Slope of 3 and distance-to-prey of 3 contribute the same to the final suitability decision, which is almost certainly wrong. Distance to roads, in particular, is likely a much stronger predictor of Puma habitat use than the current weighting reflects.

Land cover is static. The NLCD layer is a single 2021 snapshot. Habitat suitability is not static — fire regimes, drought, and land use change all reshape it year over year. A time-series version would be more useful, but requires more data processing than this project’s scope allowed.

What This Project Actually Demonstrates

Fuzzy inference is a genuinely good fit for ecological modeling. Habitat suitability is inherently gradual, and forcing it into binary or categorical bins throws away information that the model could otherwise use. The membership functions in this project encode a specific expert-informed theory about what makes good mountain lion habitat, and because that theory is written down in readable form, it can be argued with, revised, or replaced.

The rule base is where the real work lives. Rewriting it with expert-informed variable weights — instead of the uniform preference scoring used here — would be the single highest-leverage next step. The rest of the pipeline is solid.

The protected-area finding is the piece that I’d want to see extended. Comparing suitability maps against protected area boundaries is a well-known technique in conservation planning, and the 46% unsuitable figure here aligns with what’s been documented in other regions. It’s not a novel result, but arriving at it independently with a different model class and a different study area is at least a consistency check.

The full methodology, rule base, and processed rasters are available in the project repository.
