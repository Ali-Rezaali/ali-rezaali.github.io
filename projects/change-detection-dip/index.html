Digital Image Processing — Histograms, Thresholding, and Change Detection

Two laboratory reports in classical image processing: threshold-based segmentation of an urban traffic scene, and multi-date change detection on aerial imagery of an aircraft boneyard.

#Image Processing#OpenCV#Python#Segmentation#Change Detection#Thresholding

Overview

Two reports from the Digital Image Processing course at K. N. Toosi University of Technology. Both use classical techniques: histograms, thresholding, connected-component labeling, and template operations. No deep learning, no pretrained models.

The first part works on a single urban traffic photograph: separate asphalt from vegetation, isolate road markings, and count vehicles by pixel-area footprint. The second part compares two aerial images of the same airfield taken three weeks apart, a change detection problem solved through histogram matching and threshold masking.

Both notebooks are available to run:

  • Part 1 — Histogram analysis and thresholding
  • Part 2 — Histogram equalization and matching

Part 1 — Traffic Scene Segmentation

The scene is an overhead photograph of a multi-lane intersection with cars, buses, road markings, asphalt, and vegetation. The task: extract road markings and vehicles using color features and geometry, without any trained model.

Step 1 — Color channel analysis

The first question is which channel carries the signal worth working on.

import cv2
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from skimage.io import imshow, imread
from skimage.measure import label, regionprops, regionprops_table

traffic = imread('traffic.jpg')

color = ('r', 'g', 'b')
ColorNames = ('Red', 'Green', 'Blue')

for i, col in enumerate(color):
    plt.subplot(3, 2, 2*i + 1)
    plt.title(f'Image ({ColorNames[i]})')
    imshow(traffic[:, :, i])

    plt.subplot(3, 2, 2*i + 2)
    plt.title(f'Histogram ({ColorNames[i]})')
    hist = cv2.calcHist([traffic], [i], None, [256], [0, 256])
    plt.plot(hist, col)

The red channel has the highest contrast between asphalt (dark) and everything else: vegetation, lane markings, and vehicle bodies all sit at distinctly higher intensity values. Green and blue are flatter, with less separation. Everything below works on the red channel only.

Step 2 — Asphalt masking

Asphalt sits in a specific intensity range in the red channel. Pick those values, threshold, get a mask of the road surface.

img = traffic[:, :, 0]
asphaltmask = np.zeros_like(img)

asphaltmask[(img > 45) & (img < 49)] = 1
asphaltmask[(img > 68) & (img < 130)] = 1

asphaltmask = cv2.GaussianBlur(asphaltmask, (3, 3), cv2.BORDER_DEFAULT)

Two disjoint intervals because the histogram showed two peaks in the asphalt range: one for fresh asphalt, one for older, weathered surface. This is a heuristic, calibrated by eye against known asphalt patches. It is not a physical model.

Step 3 — Connected component analysis

Raw threshold output is noisy. skimage.measure.label turns the binary mask into connected components, and regionprops_table gives their areas, which is where filtering actually happens.

label1 = label(asphaltmask, connectivity=img.ndim)
properties = regionprops_table(label1, img, properties=['label', 'area'])
props = pd.DataFrame(properties)

Filtering by area range then selecting specific object classes. One range for the large asphalt regions, another for the medium ones, another for small fragments. The masks get combined back together:

mask2 = np.zeros_like(label1)
for i in label_ids_big:
    mask2[label1 == i] = 1
mask3 = np.zeros_like(label1)
for i in label_ids_medium:
    mask3[label1 == i] = 1

final_mask = cv2.add(mask2, mask3)
final_mask = cv2.add(final_mask, mask4)

Object area in pixels encodes object class. A large contiguous region of dark red is asphalt. A tiny one is shadow or a car window. Choosing the right intervals pulls out the surfaces wanted without needing a learned classifier.

Step 4 — Vegetation via HSV

Thresholding the red channel for vegetation fails because plants and cars overlap too much in RGB. HSV changes the problem.

hsv = cv2.cvtColor(traffic, cv2.COLOR_RGB2HSV)

h, s, v = cv2.split(hsv)
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(14, 10))
ax = fig.add_subplot(111, projection='3d')
ax.scatter(h.flatten(), s.flatten(), v.flatten(), facecolors=pixel_colors, marker='.')
ax.set_xlabel('Hue')
ax.set_ylabel('Saturation')
ax.set_zlabel('Value')

The 3D scatter of HSV values shows the vegetation cluster sitting in a compact region of the hue axis that no other class occupies. Selecting it:

range_min = (14, 42, 0)
range_max = (45, 240, 180)
mask = cv2.inRange(hsv, range_min, range_max)

This worked far better than any RGB-based vegetation threshold.

Step 5 — Road markings and vehicles

Lane markings and pedestrian crossing lines come from the inverse of the asphalt mask, filtered by area. Bright, elongated regions surrounded by road surface.

Vehicles: after removing asphalt and vegetation, what remains is mostly car bodies and shadows. Region props filtered by area give a rough vehicle mask.

  • Large blobs (~2000-8000 px) → buses, trucks
  • Medium blobs (~300-1500 px) → cars, vans
  • Tiny blobs → noise

The result segments roughly the right regions but with a consistent failure: cars parked adjacent to lane markings merge with the marking mask, so they are not counted as vehicles. Cars in motion create motion blur that changes their apparent area.

The bonus section asked for three classes (sedan, van, bus). Sizes did stratify along those lines, but the boundaries were set by eye and would not generalize to another image at another altitude.

What failed

Three approaches did not work.

Template matching. Sliding a crop of a single car across the image and finding every match failed immediately. The scene contains sedans, SUVs, buses, trucks, and vans. One template recognizes one of them. Multi-class object detection is where template matching stops and machine learning begins.

Multi-Otsu thresholding. Splitting the grayscale image into 6 classes via multi-Otsu produced regions that did not correspond to semantic classes. The histogram does not have enough separation to support that many thresholds, and multi-Otsu assumes roughly equal-mass bins, which the traffic scene violates.

Empty-scene subtraction. The cleanest way to isolate vehicles would be to subtract an image of the same road without cars. That image does not exist and could not be fabricated. Photoshop cleanup of the original was too lossy to use. Without a clean background plate, this approach is unavailable.

The pipeline works at a coarse level for a single image with known calibration. It is not a vehicle detector.

Part 2 — Multi-Date Change Detection

Two aerial photographs of the same airfield in Victorville, California, taken three weeks apart. Aircraft moved between acquisitions, some from parking rows onto taxiways, some between ramp positions. The task is to detect and isolate those changes.

The two images were flown on different dates under different lighting, so the first problem is not change detection. It is making the two images comparable.

Secondary Image

Step 1 — Loading and channel comparison

ref = cv2.imread('victorville_planes_0000_20200405_ssc12-1-full.jpg')
ref = cv2.cvtColor(ref, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB)

img = cv2.imread('victorville_planes_0002_20200329_ssc1-3-full.jpg')
img = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB)

Per-channel histograms of both images show a brightness offset. The reference is systematically brighter than the second image by 5 to 15 grayscale levels, more in red than in green or blue. This is an illumination difference, not a content difference. Raw subtraction would produce a global false-positive signal.

Reference Image and Second Image with their RGB Channels’ Histograms

Step 2 — Histogram equalization

eq_ref = np.zeros_like(ref)
eq_img = np.zeros_like(img)

for c in range(3):
    eq_ref[:, :, c] = cv.equalizeHist(ref[:, :, c])
    eq_img[:, :, c] = cv.equalizeHist(img[:, :, c])

Per-channel equalization spreads each histogram across the full 0-255 range, giving both images the same statistical character. This is a global operation. It does not align the two images specifically, it just normalizes both toward a uniform distribution. Useful as preprocessing, not ideal for change detection, because the operation is scene-dependent. The same pixel intensity in the two images may map to different output values if the underlying content distribution differs.

Reference Image and Second Image with their RGB Channels’ Equalized Histograms

Equalized Reference and Second Images

Step 3 — Histogram matching

Instead of normalizing both images to a common uniform shape, transform one image so its histogram matches the other.

from skimage.exposure import match_histograms

matched = match_histograms(img, ref, multichannel=True)

matched now has the same pixel-value distribution as ref, so direct subtraction isolates true content changes rather than illumination differences. The before/after histograms confirm the alignment. The matched histogram sits on top of the reference histogram.

Step 4 — Absolute difference

diff = cv2.absdiff(matched, ref)

The difference image is dominated by black (no change), with bright regions where content actually moved. Most of the signal is concentrated in the aircraft parking rows, where aircraft were repositioned between the two dates.

The aircraft rows that were already there in both images still show up faintly in the difference, because their shadows and surface reflections change slightly between passes. No two acquisitions of the same scene are perfectly identical.

Step 5 — Thresholding the difference

The diff image has a long tail of low-amplitude noise and a small number of high-amplitude change pixels. Two methods were applied.

Adaptive mean and adaptive Gaussian: threshold varies locally based on neighborhood statistics. Works well on scenes with uneven illumination, but produces a lot of speckle in regions of near-constant difference.

Otsu: picks a single global threshold by maximizing inter-class variance in the histogram.

Conv_hsv_Gray = cv2.cvtColor(difference, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
ret, mask = cv2.threshold(
    Conv_hsv_Gray, 0, 255,
    cv2.THRESH_BINARY_INV | cv2.THRESH_OTSU
)
difference[mask == 255] = [0, 0, 255]

Thresholding Results Using Global Thresholding, Adaptive Mean Thresholding, Adaptive Gaussian Thresholding

Otsu worked better here. The difference histogram is clearly bimodal (change vs no-change), which is the shape Otsu is designed for. The resulting mask isolated the moved aircraft cleanly, with the faint shadow artifacts from Step 4 correctly excluded.

Step 6 — Counting changes

The final mask went through the same label + regionprops_table workflow as Part 1. Small-area components were treated as noise, medium-area components as individual aircraft moves. This gave a rough count of how many aircraft had been repositioned between the two dates.

The sequence (two images at different illumination, aligned histograms, difference, threshold, labeled change regions) shows up in crop monitoring, urban growth detection, and glacier retreat measurement. Same building blocks.

Notes

Two things carry over from both parts.

Channel selection is a modeling decision. The traffic scene has far better contrast between asphalt and everything else in red than in green or blue. The aircraft boneyard’s change signal was cleanest after histogram matching, not before. Choosing the right representation often matters more than choosing the right algorithm.

Thresholding is fragile by design. Every threshold here was calibrated by eye against a single image. Move the camera, change the lighting, swap the scene, and every number needs retuning. That is not a bug. It is the limitation of classical methods, and it is why the field moved to learned representations.
